Physics describes systems and processes. Processes can follow one another, and systems can be put together. Category theory records these operations and the relations between them. This course develops the structures needed to describe antiparticles, fusion and exchange, boundaries, and topological quantum field theories. Its examples are finite enough to calculate.

The notes are intended for independent reading. Lecture numbers divide the material into topics. A lecture need not be read in one sitting. The worked examples and exercises are part of the learning path. There is no page or reading-time target.

Starting knowledge

The course assumes linear algebra over C\CC, including dual spaces and tensor products. It also assumes group representations, intertwiners, Schur’s lemma, characters and the addition of angular momenta. The relevant background is in Linear Algebra for Physics, Lectures 11–12, Symmetries, Lectures 3–5, 8 and 12–14, and Quantum Mechanics I, Lectures 10–11 and 13.

The optional diagnostic, Sheet 0, turns these prerequisites into tasks. Its questions identify the earlier lectures that supply each tool and the places where it is used here. Difficulty with dual bases points back to linear algebra. Difficulty with characters or spin multiplicities points back to representation theory. Difficulty with partial traces points back to quantum tensor products. The diagnostic has hints, answers and worked solutions. Its purpose is to identify what to revisit.

No category theory, Hopf algebras or knot theory is assumed. The topology used in the examples is introduced through paths, surfaces and gluing. The XXZ comparison in Lecture 8 is optional context for readers of Integrability. The chain and lattice models used later are described where their properties are needed.

The route through the course

The first block develops the language. Lectures 1 and 2 describe familiar linear algebra and representations by their maps. A linear category alone forgets how representations combine. Lecture 3 adds a tensor product, and Lecture 4 adds duals and traces. The first practical task is to turn a composite of maps into a diagram and read its source and target back.

The second block describes fusion and exchange. Lecture 5 constructs fusion spaces from allowed intermediate charges. Lecture 6 changes the fusion basis, and Lecture 7 computes exchanges. Fibonacci is the main calculation: the same particles lead to a fusion rule, an F-matrix, R-symbols and a braid operation on a two-dimensional fusion space.

The third block constructs examples. Lecture 8 explains how an algebra can supply tensor products, duals and braidings on its representations. Lecture 9 develops ribbons, link evaluation and the finite categories SU(2)k\SU(2)_kSU(2)k​. These constructions explain where several of the earlier models come from. The ribbon structure in the first section of Lecture 9 is also used by Lecture 10. A route focused on anyon data must retain that section even when treating the quantum-group and Temperley–Lieb constructions as further study.

The fourth block, Lecture 10, asks which charges can be detected by braiding. It constructs the S-matrix, recovers the fusion rules and computes central charges. The fifth block, Lectures 11 to 13, studies actions on categories. The two small actions of VecZ2\catname{Vec}_{\Z_2}VecZ2​​ eventually describe the two boundaries of the toric code. The centre describes the bulk, while a dual category describes boundary excitations. These categories have different roles.

The last block, Lecture 14, relates gluing spaces and spacetimes to linear maps. Duality reappears in one dimension, a commutative Frobenius algebra describes two-dimensional theories, and fusion and modular data supply three-dimensional constructions. The distinction between a state space and a partition function remains essential.

Examples to keep together

The examples have different purposes. The representations of S3S_3S3​ show which information a categorical structure remembers. The categories VecGω\VecG[\omega]VecGω​ show how an associator changes without changing fusion rules. Fibonacci supplies explicit many-particle calculations. Ising and SU(2)2\SU(2)_2SU(2)2​ show why equal fusion rules need not give the same associators or spins. The centre of VecZ2\catname{Vec}_{\Z_2}VecZ2​​ connects a small algebraic calculation to the toric code and its boundaries.

Practice and feedback

After a worked example, reproduce its main construction with the notes closed. Check the types of maps and the allowed labels before doing arithmetic. For a picture, write its source and target and name the rule that permits the next move. A correct final number does not identify a mistaken intermediate step.

Each lecture has a sheet of longer exercises. Every exercise has a hint, an answer and a complete worked method. The three PDF editions contain questions only, hints and answers, or worked solutions. On the website the stages open separately. Attempt the question first. Use a hint to restart, compare the answer, then read the worked method to locate a gap. The starred exercise is an extension. Ordinary exercises do not depend on solving it.

The website also has six short self-checks per lecture. These have explained answers. The companion Self-checks with Explained Answers prints the same questions, with all feedback after the questions. What a proof label means

An unqualified theorem or proposition is proved with the tools stated in its proof. The label proved given coherence records the use of the coherence theorem behind strict pictures. A sketch gives the central argument without every detail. A cited result is an input whose proof is in the stated source. A physical principle identifies a physical assumption rather than a mathematical theorem. An example explains how an input is used; it does not change that input’s status.

Compound labels distinguish the general input from the part established here. The label proved by verification identifies a finite identity check; the proof states which data and identities are covered.

Diagram reference

A diagram denotes a morphism with specified input and output. In a process diagram, time runs upward. Read inputs from left to right along the bottom, and outputs from left to right along the top. An orientation arrow on a strand distinguishes an object from its dual. It does not reverse the direction in which the composite is read. A spatial lattice or a surface drawing is identified as such in its caption.

Composition and tensor product

A box f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y followed by g ⁣:Y→Zg\colon Y\to Zg:Y→Z denotes g∘f ⁣:X→Zg\circ f\colon X\to Zg∘f:X→Z. Two boxes beside one another denote a tensor product. The pictures below have types X→ZX\to ZX→Z and X⊗U→Y⊗VX\otimes U\to Y\otimes VX⊗U→Y⊗V, respectively.

The composite has fff below ggg. In the tensor product the first factor is the left strand. Boxes on independent strands can change their relative height by interchange (Theorem 3.4). This does not exchange the strands.

The unit and scalars

The tensor unit is drawn by the absence of a strand. A scalar is a morphism 1→11\to11→1, so its box has no incident strands. Scalars commute with one another (Theorem 3.6). In a general monoidal category a scalar can slide on the same side of a strand, but cannot be moved to the other side merely by a planar deformation. In the linear fusion categories used for the anyon calculations, a scalar is a complex number and floats on either side. These statements have different hypotheses.

Bends and duality

A cup has type 1→X⊗X∗1\to X\otimes X^{*}1→X⊗X∗ and a cap has type X∗⊗X→1X^{*}\otimes X\to1X∗⊗X→1. Their snake composites are identities (Definition 4.1). Before straightening a bend, check the order of its labels and the chosen cap. The four-bend atlas in Lecture 4 (Figure 4.1) also includes the pivotal closing maps, which have the opposite order. Turning a box through a half-turn with the stated dual bends gives f∗ ⁣:Y∗→X∗f^{*}\colon Y^{*}\to X^{*}f∗:Y∗→X∗ (Theorem 4.2). In chosen dual bases this is the transpose matrix. In a dagger category, reflecting the picture gives f† ⁣:Y→Xf^{\dagger}\colon Y\to Xf†:Y→X. In finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces its matrix in orthonormal bases is the conjugate transpose. These are different operations.

Pivotal and spherical closures

A pivotal structure supplies the comparison with the double dual needed to close a strand. The resulting trace is cyclic. Its value on the identity is the quantum dimension (Definition 4.6 and Theorem 4.7). Name the pivotal structure when assigning a number to a loop. A spherical structure makes the two trace closures agree. Neither assertion licenses replacing an arbitrary cap by a dagger cap. A flag on a self-dual bend records the self-duality identification; its reversal may produce the Frobenius–Schur sign (Definition 4.9).

Vertices and F-moves

A splitting vertex is a map c→a⊗bc\to a\otimes bc→a⊗b and its dagger is the fusion vertex. The bubble and completeness relations have the coefficients printed in Equations (5.1) and (5.3). They depend on the chosen vertex normalization. A fusion tree also specifies a basis vector in a fusion space with fixed external and total charges.

An F-move expands one tree basis in another (Definition 6.1). The two branching graphs are different. An F-move is not a planar deformation and does not measure the intermediate charge. Use the inverse basis change when returning to the original basis. The three-particle calculation in Lecture 7 distinguishes these basis changes from the physical exchange between them.

Crossings and ribbons

The crossing cX,Y ⁣:X⊗Y→Y⊗Xc_{X,Y}\colon X\otimes Y\to Y\otimes XcX,Y​:X⊗Y→Y⊗X puts the strand from bottom left to top right over the other. A crossing followed by its inverse cancels by invertibility, the Reidemeister II move. A double crossing of the same handedness does not generally cancel. Naturality lets a box pass through a crossing, and the hexagons imply Reidemeister III (Definition 7.2 and Theorem 7.4). A strand passing over a pair can be expanded as a crossing over each strand by the hexagon.

A ribbon remembers its framing. A curl equals a twist on a straight strand, not an unchanged strand (Equation (9.2)). The twist is a specified endomorphism. Removing it would discard information. The marked-band picture in Lecture 9 shows the framing that the thin-strand notation records.

Walls, surfaces and spatial graphs

A module-category picture has a region for a category and a wall for one of its objects. It is not a fusion vertex between three bulk particles. Its joining rules are the module action and module associator (Definition 11.3). Keep the bulk, the module category and the category of boundary excitations separate, as in the role diagram of Lecture 12 and the boundary table of Lecture 13.

In a bordism picture the input and output are actual boundary spaces. A circle can represent a space assigned a vector space, whereas a closed surface can represent a spacetime assigned a number. The surface-to-map dictionary of Lecture 14 states the dimension of the theory. In a string-net picture the graph lies in a spatial surface; the F relation changes the local graph description. The state-sum figure instead depicts a triangulation of spacetime. Its tetrahedron labels and normalization are additional data, specified by the construction.
