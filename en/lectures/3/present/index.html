Lecture 3

Monoidal categories and string diagrams

Lecture 2 showed that a semisimple category alone cannot tell Rep(S3)\Rep(S_3)Rep(S3​) from Rep(Z3)\Rep(\Z_3)Rep(Z3​): what can is the tensor product. This lecture adds it. Putting systems side by side is a functor ⊗\otimes⊗ with an associator and unitors, subject to two axioms, the pentagon and the triangle. Coherence says that these axioms are all one needs, and string diagrams turn it into a calculus of pictures. Associators need not be trivial: on Z2\Z_2Z2​ there are two monoidal categories with the same fusion rules and different associators.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • State the definition of a monoidal category, check it on Vect\VectVect, Rep(G)\Rep(G)Rep(G) and the matrices, and draw the pentagon and the triangle.
  • Read and draw string diagrams, prove the interchange law, and use sliding and the commutativity of scalars in pictures.
  • Show that the pentagon of VecGω\VecG[\omega]VecGω​ is the 333-cocycle condition, and solve it for Z2\Z_2Z2​.
  • Define a monoidal functor, and show that ω(s,s,s)=±1\omega(s,s,s)=\pm1ω(s,s,s)=±1 cannot be changed by one that is the identity on objects.

3.1 The definition

For objects of Vect\VectVect, (U⊗V)⊗W(U\otimes V)\otimes W(U⊗V)⊗W and U⊗(V⊗W)U\otimes(V\otimes W)U⊗(V⊗W) are different spaces, identified by (u⊗v)⊗w↦u⊗(v⊗w)(u\otimes v)\otimes w\mapsto u\otimes(v\otimes w)(u⊗v)⊗w↦u⊗(v⊗w). A monoidal category keeps track of such identifications.

Definition 3.1 (Monoidal category). A monoidal category is a category C\cat{C}C with

  • a tensor product: an object X⊗YX\otimes YX⊗Y for all objects and a morphism f⊗g ⁣:X⊗Y→X′⊗Y′f\otimes g\colon X\otimes Y\to X'\otimes Y'f⊗g:X⊗Y→X′⊗Y′ for all f ⁣:X→X′f\colon X\to X'f:X→X′ and g ⁣:Y→Y′g\colon Y\to Y'g:Y→Y′, with id⁡X⊗id⁡Y=id⁡X⊗Y\id_X\otimes\id_Y=\id_{X\otimes Y}idX​⊗idY​=idX⊗Y​ and (g⊗k)∘(f⊗h)=(g∘f)⊗(k∘h);\begin{aligned} &(g\otimes k)\circ(f\otimes h)\\ &\quad=(g\circ f)\otimes(k\circ h); \end{aligned}​(g⊗k)∘(f⊗h)=(g∘f)⊗(k∘h);​

  • a unit object 111;

  • natural isomorphisms, the associator and the unitors, αX,Y,Z ⁣:(X⊗Y)⊗Z→X⊗(Y⊗Z),lX ⁣:1⊗X→X,rX ⁣:X⊗1→X,\begin{gathered} \alpha_{X,Y,Z}\colon(X\otimes Y)\otimes Z\\ \to X\otimes(Y\otimes Z),\\ l_X\colon 1\otimes X\to X,\\ r_X\colon X\otimes 1\to X, \end{gathered}αX,Y,Z​:(X⊗Y)⊗Z→X⊗(Y⊗Z),lX​:1⊗X→X,rX​:X⊗1→X,​

such that the pentagon and the triangle commute for all objects: It is strict if α\alphaα, lll and rrr are identities.

The first condition says that ⊗\otimes⊗ is a functor of two variables; we return to it in §3.3.

3.2 Coherence (formal diagrams)

In W⊗X⊗Y⊗ZW\otimes X\otimes Y\otimes ZW⊗X⊗Y⊗Z there are five bracketings and many composites of associators between them. The pentagon equates two of them; Mac Lane’s theorem says this is enough.

Theorem 3.3 (Coherence cited).

  1. (a)

    Every monoidal category is monoidally equivalent (§3.6) to a strict one.

  2. (b)

    Take a word x1⊗⋯⊗xnx_1\otimes\dots\otimes x_nx1​⊗⋯⊗xn​ in formal variables. Any two formal morphisms between two of its bracketings, built from α\alphaα, lll, rrr, their inverses and identities by ∘\circ∘ and ⊗\otimes⊗, are equal, and stay equal when objects of C\cat{C}C are put in for the variables.

(Mac Lane, §VII.2 and §XI.3; EGNO Thms. 2.8.5 and 2.9.2.)

What must be formal is the morphism, not just its source and target. Mac Lane points out that two formally different bracketings may become the same object, and then a morphism between them need not be formal. The category VecGω\VecG[\omega]VecGω​ on Z2\Z_2Z2​ has simple objects 111 and sss, with s⊗s=1s\otimes s=1s⊗s=1 (§3.5). For ω(s,s,s)=−1\omega(s,s,s)=-1ω(s,s,s)=−1, (s⊗s)⊗s=s=s⊗(s⊗s),(s\otimes s)\otimes s=s=s\otimes(s\otimes s),(s⊗s)⊗s=s=s⊗(s⊗s), yet αs,s,s=−id⁡s\alpha_{s,s,s}=-\id_sαs,s,s​=−ids​: the diagram formed by αs,s,s\alpha_{s,s,s}αs,s,s​ and id⁡s\id_sids​ does not commute. There is no contradiction: id⁡s\id_sids​ compares the two bracketings only because they happen to be the same object, and it is not a formal morphism from (x⊗x)⊗x(x\otimes x)\otimes x(x⊗x)⊗x to x⊗(x⊗x)x\otimes(x\otimes x)x⊗(x⊗x). Some textbooks state coherence as “every diagram commutes”; that is false.

What (a) and (b) give us: in a formal computation the brackets and the associators can be dropped, and we write X⊗Y⊗ZX\otimes Y\otimes ZX⊗Y⊗Z.

3.3 String diagrams; interchange; Joyal–Street

In a strict monoidal category the pictures of Lecture 1 acquire a second direction. A morphism f ⁣:X1⊗⋯⊗Xm→Y1⊗⋯⊗Ynf\colon X_1\otimes\dots\otimes X_m\to Y_1\otimes\dots \otimes Y_nf:X1​⊗⋯⊗Xm​→Y1​⊗⋯⊗Yn​ is a box with mmm strands entering from below and nnn leaving above, the first factor on the left. Composition stacks boxes upward, ⊗\otimes⊗ places pictures side by side, id⁡X\id_XidX​ is a strand, and the unit 111 is the empty picture: it has no strand. For f ⁣:X⊗Y→Zf\colon X\otimes Y\to Zf:X⊗Y→Z and g ⁣:U→Vg\colon U\to Vg:U→V, (3.1) a morphism X⊗Y⊗U→Z⊗VX\otimes Y\otimes U\to Z\otimes VX⊗Y⊗U→Z⊗V.

Theorem 3.4 (Interchange). For composable f,gf,gf,g and composable h,kh,kh,k, (g⊗k)∘(f⊗h)=(g∘f)⊗(k∘h).\begin{aligned} &(g\otimes k)\circ(f\otimes h)\\ &\quad=(g\circ f)\otimes(k\circ h). \end{aligned}​(g⊗k)∘(f⊗h)=(g∘f)⊗(k∘h).​

Proof. This is the first condition of Definition 3.1: ⊗\otimes⊗ is a functor on pairs of morphisms, which compose slot by slot. 

The theorem is what makes a picture unambiguous. The diagram (3.2) can be read row by row or column by column, and the two readings agree. As a special case, boxes on different strands slide past each other: (3.3) that is, (id⁡⊗h)∘(f⊗id⁡)=f⊗h=(f⊗id⁡)∘(id⁡⊗h).\begin{aligned} (\id\otimes h)\circ(f\otimes\id)&=f\otimes h\\ &=(f\otimes\id)\circ(\id\otimes h). \end{aligned}(id⊗h)∘(f⊗id)​=f⊗h=(f⊗id)∘(id⊗h).​ The heights of boxes on different strands carry no meaning.

A first proof by pictures: the action of GGG on V⊗WV\otimes WV⊗W in Example 3.2 is a representation. Writing ggg for ρV(g)\rho_V(g)ρV​(g) and for ρW(g)\rho_W(g)ρW​(g), interchange and ρ(g)ρ(h)=ρ(gh)\rho(g)\rho(h)=\rho(gh)ρ(g)ρ(h)=ρ(gh) on each strand give (3.4) that is, (ρV(g)⊗ρW(g))(ρV(h)⊗ρW(h))=ρV(gh)⊗ρW(gh).\begin{aligned} &(\rho_V(g)\otimes\rho_W(g))(\rho_V(h)\otimes\rho_W(h))\\ &\quad=\rho_V(gh)\otimes\rho_W(gh). \end{aligned}​(ρV​(g)⊗ρW​(g))(ρV​(h)⊗ρW​(h))=ρV​(gh)⊗ρW​(gh).​

Theorem 3.5 (Joyal–Street cited). In a strict monoidal category, two string diagrams that can be deformed into one another in the plane, keeping the ends of the strands fixed, never letting strands cross, and never letting a strand turn downward, denote the same morphism (Heunen–Vicary Thm. 1.8; Selinger Thm. 3.1).

By coherence (Theorem 3.3) the theorem applies to any monoidal category: a picture determines the morphism once its source and target are bracketed, and by (b) every way of inserting associators to match them gives the same result. Quantum Computing’s soundness theorem for circuits (L2, Thm. 2.2) is the case of MatC\catname{Mat}_{\C}MatC​, read a quarter turn round.

3.5 VecGω\VecG[\omega]VecGω​: the Z2\Z_2Z2​ pentagon

The associator is data. The simplest place to see that it can be non-trivial is a category whose simple objects form a group.

3.6 Monoidal functors

Summary

  • Handed on: a monoidal category is (C,⊗,1,α,l,r)(\cat{C},\otimes,1,\alpha,l,r)(C,⊗,1,α,l,r) with the pentagon and the triangle (Definition 3.1); by coherence, formal diagrams commute and brackets can be dropped (Theorem 3.3), but non-formal ones need not.
  • Pictures: boxes and strands with time upward; interchange makes them unambiguous, boxes on different strands slide, and planar deformations preserve the morphism (Theorems 3.4 and 3.5).
  • Scalars commute (Theorem 3.6).
  • The main example: in VecGω\VecG[\omega]VecGω​ the pentagon is the 333-cocycle condition; on Z2\Z_2Z2​, ω(s,s,s)=±1\omega(s,s,s)=\pm1ω(s,s,s)=±1, and no monoidal functor that is the identity on objects relates the two (Theorem 3.8 and Proposition 3.11).
  • Next: Lecture 4 adds duals: cups, caps and loops.
