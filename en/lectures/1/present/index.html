Lecture 1

Categories: a theory of systems and processes

Physics composes processes: one after another, and, from Lecture 3 on, side by side. This lecture fixes the vocabulary that every later lecture uses: categories, functors, natural transformations and equivalences. Its main result says that a representation of a group is a functor, and an intertwiner a natural transformation. Naturality then makes “canonical” precise: V→V∗∗V\to V^{**}V→V∗∗ is natural, and no linear isomorphism V→V∗V\to V^{*}V→V∗ is.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Check the axioms of a category on an example, and read a group as a category with one object.
  • Prove that a representation of GGG is a functor BG→VectfdBG\to\VectfdBG→Vectfd​ and an intertwiner a natural transformation, and slide a box through a natural transformation in a picture.
  • Prove that V→V∗∗V\to V^{**}V→V∗∗ is natural and that no family of linear isomorphisms V→V∗V\to V^{*}V→V∗ is, and say why the Riesz map escapes.
  • Prove Vectfd≃MatC\Vectfd\simeq\catname{Mat}_{\C}Vectfd​≃MatC​, and explain why the two categories are equivalent but not isomorphic.

1.1 Systems and processes

Three theories from this collection share a shape. In quantum mechanics the systems are Hilbert spaces and the processes linear maps. In representation theory the systems are representations of a group GGG and the processes are intertwiners (Symmetries, Lecture 3). In topology the systems are closed curves, finitely many circles, and a process is a surface whose boundary is the incoming circles and the outgoing ones, a cobordism. The pair of pants takes two circles to one: We draw time upward, as throughout the course: a process starts at the bottom and ends at the top.

In each theory a process has an input and an output. Two processes compose when the output of the first is the input of the second. Composing is associative. Every system has a process that does nothing: the identity map, the identity intertwiner, the cylinder. Baez and Stay set the dictionary out in a table (their Table 2). A theory with this shape is a category.

What the vocabulary buys is a way to compare theories. A functor translates one category into another, and a natural transformation compares two translations. The main result (Theorem 1.9) says that a representation of GGG is exactly a functor from a category with one object, and an intertwiner exactly a natural transformation. Two further results show what naturality is for: it makes the word “canonical” a theorem (Theorem 1.11), and it lets two categories be “the same” without being equal (Theorem 1.16).

1.2 Categories and examples

Definition 1.1 (Category). A category C\cat{C}C consists of

  • for each pair of objects a set Hom⁡(X,Y)\Hom(X,Y)Hom(X,Y) of morphisms f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y;

Definition 1.3 (Isomorphism, groupoid). A morphism f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y is an isomorphism if some g ⁣:Y→Xg\colon Y\to Xg:Y→X has g∘f=id⁡X,f∘g=id⁡Y.\begin{gathered} g\circ f=\id_X,\\ f\circ g=\id_Y . \end{gathered}g∘f=idX​,f∘g=idY​.​ Then ggg is unique, written f−1f^{-1}f−1, and X≅YX\cong YX≅Y. A groupoid is a category in which every morphism is an isomorphism.

Uniqueness: if hhh is another such morphism, then h=h∘(f∘g)=(h∘f)∘g=g.h=h\circ(f\circ g)=(h\circ f)\circ g=g .h=h∘(f∘g)=(h∘f)∘g=g. The isomorphisms of Vect\VectVect are the invertible linear maps. The finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces with only the unitary maps form a groupoid; with all linear maps they form Hilbfd\Hilb_{\mathrm{fd}}Hilbfd​, which is not one.

1.4 Natural transformations; Rep(G)≅Fun(BG,Vectfd)\Rep(G)\cong\Fun(BG,\Vectfd)Rep(G)≅Fun(BG,Vectfd​)

Definition 1.7 (Natural transformation). Let F,F′ ⁣:C→DF,F'\colon\cat{C}\to\cat{D}F,F′:C→D be functors. A natural transformation ξ ⁣:F⇒F′\xi\colon F\Rightarrow F'ξ:F⇒F′ is a morphism ξX ⁣:F(X)→F′(X)\xi_X\colon F(X)\to F'(X)ξX​:F(X)→F′(X) for each object XXX, its component, such that for every f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y F′(f)∘ξX=ξY∘F(f).F'(f)\circ\xi_X=\xi_Y\circ F(f).F′(f)∘ξX​=ξY​∘F(f). It is a natural isomorphism if every component is an isomorphism.

The condition says that the square commutes: the two paths from F(X)F(X)F(X) to F′(Y)F'(Y)F′(Y) agree. Drawn upward, it says that the box F(f)F(f)F(f) slides through ξ\xiξ and comes out as F′(f)F'(f)F′(f): (1.2) The square names the objects at its corners; the picture puts them on strands and lets the boxes move. From Lecture 3 on we use the picture, because it extends to two dimensions.

Lemma 1.8 (Composing natural transformations). If ξ ⁣:F⇒F′\xi\colon F\Rightarrow F'ξ:F⇒F′ and ζ ⁣:F′⇒F′′\zeta\colon F'\Rightarrow F''ζ:F′⇒F′′ are natural, so is ζ∘ξ ⁣:F⇒F′′\zeta\circ\xi\colon F\Rightarrow F''ζ∘ξ:F⇒F′′, with components (ζ∘ξ)X=ζX∘ξX.(\zeta\circ\xi)_X=\zeta_X\circ\xi_X .(ζ∘ξ)X​=ζX​∘ξX​.

Proof. Slide F(f)F(f)F(f) through ξ\xiξ by (1.2), then F′(f)F'(f)F′(f) through ζ\zetaζ by (1.2) for ζ\zetaζ: (1.3) The outer pictures are the two sides of naturality for ζ∘ξ\zeta\circ\xiζ∘ξ. 

If the objects of C\cat{C}C form a set, as for BGBGBG, the functors C→D\cat{C}\to\cat{D}C→D form a category Fun(C,D)\Fun(\cat{C},\cat{D})Fun(C,D): its morphisms are the natural transformations, composed by Lemma 1.8, with identities (id⁡F)X=id⁡F(X)(\id_F)_X=\id_{F(X)}(idF​)X​=idF(X)​. Associativity and the unit laws hold component by component.

Two categories are isomorphic, C≅D\cat{C}\cong\cat{D}C≅D, if there are functors C→D→C\cat{C}\to\cat{D}\to\cat{C}C→D→C whose two composites are the identity functors.

Theorem 1.9 (Representations are functors). A functor BG→VectfdBG\to\VectfdBG→Vectfd​ is a representation of GGG, and a natural transformation between two such functors is an intertwiner. Hence Rep(G)≅Fun(BG,Vectfd),\Rep(G)\cong\Fun(BG,\Vectfd),Rep(G)≅Fun(BG,Vectfd​), an isomorphism of categories that changes no data.

Proof. A functor F ⁣:BG→VectfdF\colon BG\to\VectfdF:BG→Vectfd​ is a space V=F(∗)V=F(\ast)V=F(∗) and a linear map F(g) ⁣:V→VF(g)\colon V\to VF(g):V→V for each g∈Gg\in Gg∈G. A natural transformation ξ ⁣:F⇒F′\xi\colon F\Rightarrow F'ξ:F⇒F′ has the single component T=ξ∗ ⁣:V→V′T=\xi_\ast\colon V\to V'T=ξ∗​:V→V′, and naturality for the morphism ggg reads ρ′(g) T=Tρ(g).\rho'(g)\,T=T\rho(g).ρ′(g)T=Tρ(g). 1.5 Equivalence; V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗ vs V∗V^{*}V∗

Linear Algebra (Thm. 11.17) calls V→V∗∗V\to V^{**}V→V∗∗ canonical because it uses no basis; naturality makes this a theorem. To compare VVV with V∗V^{*}V∗, we must first make the dual covariant. On the groupoid of finite-dimensional spaces and linear isomorphisms, set D(V)=V∗,D(f)=(f∗)−1.\begin{gathered} D(V)=V^{*},\\ D(f)=(f^{*})^{-1}. \end{gathered}D(V)=V∗,D(f)=(f∗)−1.​ This is a functor, since ((g∘f)∗)−1=(g∗)−1∘(f∗)−1.\bigl((g\circ f)^{*}\bigr)^{-1}=(g^{*})^{-1}\circ(f^{*})^{-1}.((g∘f)∗)−1=(g∗)−1∘(f∗)−1.

Theorem 1.11 compares objects inside one category. The last question compares two categories. Definition 1.14 (Equivalence). An equivalence C≃D\cat{C}\simeq\cat{D}C≃D is a pair of functors F ⁣:C→DF\colon\cat{C}\to\cat{D}F:C→D and G ⁣:D→CG\colon\cat{D}\to\cat{C}G:D→C with natural isomorphisms G∘F≅id⁡C,F∘G≅id⁡D.\begin{gathered} G\circ F\cong\id_{\cat{C}},\\ F\circ G\cong\id_{\cat{D}}. \end{gathered}G∘F≅idC​,F∘G≅idD​.​

A functor F ⁣:C→DF\colon\cat{C}\to\cat{D}F:C→D is faithful or full if each map Hom⁡(X,Y)→Hom⁡(F(X),F(Y))\Hom(X,Y)\to\Hom(F(X),F(Y))Hom(X,Y)→Hom(F(X),F(Y)) is injective or surjective, respectively. Example 1.18 (Equivalent, not isomorphic). Vectfd\VectfdVectfd​ and MatC\catname{Mat}_{\C}MatC​ are not isomorphic. The line {(z,0)}⊂C2\{(z,0)\}\subset\C^2{(z,0)}⊂C2 and C\CC are different, isomorphic objects of Vectfd\VectfdVectfd​; in the skeletal MatC\catname{Mat}_{\C}MatC​ no two different objects are isomorphic. Summary

  • Handed on: categories, functors, natural transformations and equivalences; pictures with time running upward, in which a chain of boxes needs no brackets, (1.1).
  • The main result: a representation is a functor BG→VectfdBG\to\VectfdBG→Vectfd​ and an intertwiner a natural transformation, so Rep(G)≅Fun(BG,Vectfd)\Rep(G)\cong\Fun(BG,\Vectfd)Rep(G)≅Fun(BG,Vectfd​) (Theorem 1.9); in pictures, naturality is sliding, (1.2).
  • Canonical, made precise: V→V∗∗V\to V^{**}V→V∗∗ is natural, and no linear isomorphism V→V∗V\to V^{*}V→V∗ is, even for unitaries (Theorem 1.11). The Riesz map escapes only by being antilinear (Example 1.13).
  • The guard: Vectfd≃MatC\Vectfd\simeq\catname{Mat}_{\C}Vectfd​≃MatC​, but the two are not isomorphic (Theorem 1.16 and Example 1.18).
  • Next: Lecture 2 characterises ⊕\oplus⊕ and ⊗\otimes⊗ by universal properties, and shows why Rep(G)\Rep(G)Rep(G) looks like copies of Vectfd\VectfdVectfd​.
