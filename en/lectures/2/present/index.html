Lecture 2

Universal properties, adjunctions, semisimple categories

Every textbook builds V⊕WV\oplus WV⊕W and V⊗WV\otimes WV⊗W in its own way, with its own bases, and every textbook gets the same answer. This lecture says why: both are characterised by a universal property, which determines them up to a unique isomorphism compatible with the universal maps. The same idea gives the adjunction between ⊗\otimes⊗ and Hom⁡\HomHom, which Lecture 4 turns into bending wires. It then explains the shape of Rep(G)\Rep(G)Rep(G): a semisimple category is equivalent to nnn copies of Vectfd\VectfdVectfd​, one for each simple object.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • State the universal properties of products, coproducts and the tensor product, and prove that a representing object is unique up to a unique isomorphism compatible with the universal maps.
  • Prove Hom⁡(U⊗V,W)≅Hom⁡(U,Hom⁡(V,W))\Hom(U\otimes V,W)\cong\Hom(U,\Hom(V,W))Hom(U⊗V,W)≅Hom(U,Hom(V,W)), naturally, and read it as an adjunction with its unit and counit.
  • Prove that a semisimple category with simple objects X1,…,XnX_1,\dots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​ is equivalent to Vectfd n\Vectfd^{\,n}Vectfdn​, and compute its Hom⁡\HomHom spaces from multiplicities.
  • Decompose the permutation representation of S3S_3S3​, and show that a Jordan block in Rep(Z)\Rep(\Z)Rep(Z) is not a direct sum of simple objects.

2.1 Universal properties

A universal property describes an object by the morphisms into it, or out of it, rather than by its elements. The simplest kind asks for exactly one.

Definition 2.1 (Terminal and initial objects). An object TTT is terminal if every object XXX has exactly one morphism X→TX\to TX→T. It is initial if every XXX has exactly one morphism T→XT\to XT→X.

In Set\SetSet a one-point set is terminal and the empty set is initial. In Vect\VectVect the zero space is both.

Lemma 2.2. Any two terminal objects are isomorphic by a unique isomorphism, and so are any two initial objects.

Proof. Let TTT and T′T'T′ be terminal. There is exactly one morphism a ⁣:T→T′a\colon T\to T'a:T→T′ and one b ⁣:T′→Tb\colon T'\to Tb:T′→T. Then b∘ab\circ ab∘a and id⁡T\id_TidT​ are both morphisms T→TT\to TT→T, and there is only one, so b∘a=id⁡Tb\circ a=\id_Tb∘a=idT​; likewise a∘b=id⁡T′a\circ b=\id_{T'}a∘b=idT′​. The initial case is the same with the arrows reversed. 

Definition 2.3 (Product, coproduct). A product of X1X_1X1​ and X2X_2X2​ is an object PPP with morphisms pk ⁣:P→Xkp_k\colon P\to X_kpk​:P→Xk​ such that for every object ZZZ and morphisms fk ⁣:Z→Xkf_k\colon Z\to X_kfk​:Z→Xk​ there is exactly one f ⁣:Z→Pf\colon Z\to Pf:Z→P with pk∘f=fkp_k\circ f=f_kpk​∘f=fk​ for k=1,2k=1,2k=1,2: A coproduct is the same with every arrow reversed: morphisms ik ⁣:Xk→Ci_k\colon X_k\to Cik​:Xk​→C such that every pair gk ⁣:Xk→Zg_k\colon X_k\to Zgk​:Xk​→Z factors as g∘ikg\circ i_kg∘ik​ through exactly one g ⁣:C→Zg\colon C\to Zg:C→Z.

The proof of Lemma 2.2 applies word for word: a product is unique up to a unique isomorphism compatible with the projections. So “the” product is well defined, whatever construction a textbook uses. In Set\SetSet the product is X1×X2X_1\times X_2X1​×X2​ and the coproduct the disjoint union, and the two differ: for sets of 222 and 333 elements they have 666 and 555 elements. In Vect\VectVect they coincide.

Definition 2.4 (Biproduct). In a category whose Hom⁡\HomHom sets are vector spaces, composed bilinearly, a biproduct of X1X_1X1​ and X2X_2X2​ is an object X1⊕X2X_1\oplus X_2X1​⊕X2​ with morphisms ik ⁣:Xk→X1⊕X2i_k\colon X_k\to X_1\oplus X_2ik​:Xk​→X1​⊕X2​ and pk ⁣:X1⊕X2→Xkp_k\colon X_1\oplus X_2\to X_kpk​:X1​⊕X2​→Xk​ such that pk∘il=δkl id⁡Xk,i1∘p1+i2∘p2=id⁡.\begin{gathered} p_k\circ i_l=\delta_{kl}\,\id_{X_k},\\ i_1\circ p_1+i_2\circ p_2=\id . \end{gathered}pk​∘il​=δkl​idXk​​,i1​∘p1​+i2​∘p2​=id.​

The direct sum of vector spaces, with its inclusions and projections, is a biproduct, and a biproduct is both a product and a coproduct: given fk ⁣:Z→Xkf_k\colon Z\to X_kfk​:Z→Xk​, the map f=i1∘f1+i2∘f2f=i_1\circ f_1+i_2\circ f_2f=i1​∘f1​+i2​∘f2​ has pk∘f=fkp_k\circ f=f_kpk​∘f=fk​, and any fff with this property equals (i1p1+i2p2)∘f(i_1p_1+i_2p_2)\circ f(i1​p1​+i2​p2​)∘f, which is the same map. The coproduct is the mirror argument. This holds for finitely many summands only: the sequence (1,1,1,… )(1,1,1,\dots)(1,1,1,…) lies in the product of countably many copies of C\CC but not in their direct sum, whose elements have finitely many non-zero entries.

A biproduct turns morphisms into matrices. A morphism f ⁣:X1⊕X2→Y1⊕Y2f\colon X_1\oplus X_2\to Y_1\oplus Y_2f:X1​⊕X2​→Y1​⊕Y2​ is determined by its four components fjk=pj∘f∘ik ⁣:Xk→Yjf_{jk}=p_j\circ f\circ i_k\colon X_k\to Y_jfjk​=pj​∘f∘ik​:Xk​→Yj​, since f=∑j,kij∘fjk∘pk,\begin{equation} \label{eq:2:matrixarrows} f=\sum_{j,k}i_j\circ f_{jk}\circ p_k , \end{equation}f=j,k∑​ij​∘fjk​∘pk​,(2.1) and composites multiply like matrices, (g∘f)lk=∑jglj∘fjk.(g\circ f)_{lk}=\sum_j g_{lj}\circ f_{jk}.(g∘f)lk​=j∑​glj​∘fjk​. Drawn as arrows between the summands, fff is the picture and composing two such pictures sums over the paths through the middle column. We use this calculus of matrices of morphisms in §2.4.

2.2 Representables; Yoneda (statement and use)

The tensor product is not a product: Linear Algebra (Prop. 12.5) characterises it by its maps out. For every bilinear map B ⁣:U×V→WB\colon U\times V\to WB:U×V→W there is exactly one linear map B~ ⁣:U⊗V→W\widetilde B\colon U\otimes V\to WB:U⊗V→W with B~(u⊗v)=B(u,v).\widetilde B(u\otimes v)=B(u,v).B(u⊗v)=B(u,v). In other words, linear maps out of U⊗VU\otimes VU⊗V are the same as bilinear maps, naturally in WWW. Universal properties of this shape have a name.

Definition 2.5 (Representable functor). A functor Φ ⁣:C→Set\Phi\colon\cat{C}\to\SetΦ:C→Set is represented by an object XXX if there is a natural isomorphism Hom⁡(X,−)≅Φ.\Hom(X,-)\cong\Phi .Hom(X,−)≅Φ. Here Hom⁡(X,−)\Hom(X,-)Hom(X,−) sends YYY to Hom⁡(X,Y)\Hom(X,Y)Hom(X,Y) and g ⁣:Y→Y′g\colon Y\to Y'g:Y→Y′ to f↦g∘ff\mapsto g\circ ff↦g∘f.

So U⊗VU\otimes VU⊗V represents W↦{bilinear maps U×V→W}W\mapsto\{\text{bilinear maps }U\times V\to W\}W↦{bilinear maps U×V→W}, and the coproduct X1⊕X2X_1\oplus X_2X1​⊕X2​ represents Z↦Hom⁡(X1,Z)×Hom⁡(X2,Z)Z\mapsto\Hom(X_1,Z)\times\Hom(X_2,Z)Z↦Hom(X1​,Z)×Hom(X2​,Z). (The product represents a functor of the other variance, Z↦Hom⁡(Z,X1)×Hom⁡(Z,X2)Z\mapsto\Hom(Z,X_1)\times\Hom(Z,X_2)Z↦Hom(Z,X1​)×Hom(Z,X2​), by Hom⁡(−,P)\Hom(-,P)Hom(−,P); everything below holds for it with the arrows reversed.)

Theorem 2.6 (Yoneda cited). For every functor Φ ⁣:C→Set\Phi\colon\cat{C}\to\SetΦ:C→Set and object XXX, the natural transformations Hom⁡(X,−)⇒Φ\Hom(X,-)\Rightarrow\PhiHom(X,−)⇒Φ correspond bijectively to the elements of Φ(X)\Phi(X)Φ(X), by ξ↦ξX(id⁡X)\xi\mapsto\xi_X(\id_X)ξ↦ξX​(idX​) (Leinster, Thm. 4.2.1 and Cor. 4.3.3).

We need only one consequence, and we prove it directly.

Proposition 2.7 (A representing object is unique). Let ξ ⁣:Hom⁡(X,−)⇒Hom⁡(X′,−)\xi\colon\Hom(X,-)\Rightarrow\Hom(X',-)ξ:Hom(X,−)⇒Hom(X′,−) be a natural isomorphism. Proof. Naturality for f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y, applied to id⁡X\id_XidX​, gives ξY(f)=ξY(f∘id⁡X)=f∘ξX(id⁡X)=f∘φ.\begin{aligned} \xi_Y(f)&=\xi_Y(f\circ\id_X)\\ &=f\circ\xi_X(\id_X)=f\circ\varphi . \end{aligned}ξY​(f)​=ξY​(f∘idX​)=f∘ξX​(idX​)=f∘φ.​ Any φ′\varphi'φ′ with this property has φ′=ξX(id⁡X)=φ\varphi'=\xi_X(\id_X)=\varphiφ′=ξX​(idX​)=φ. Likewise ψ=ξX′−1(id⁡X′) ⁣:X→X′\psi=\xi^{-1}_{X'}(\id_{X'})\colon X\to X'ψ=ξX′−1​(idX′​):X→X′ has ξY−1(g)=g∘ψ\xi^{-1}_Y(g)=g\circ\psiξY−1​(g)=g∘ψ. If XXX and X′X'X′ both represent Φ\PhiΦ, composing one natural isomorphism with the inverse of the other gives such an ξ\xiξ. So two constructions of U⊗VU\otimes VU⊗V, or of X1⊕X2X_1\oplus X_2X1​⊕X2​, are isomorphic by exactly one isomorphism that respects the universal property. That is the precise sense in which every textbook’s tensor product is “the same”.

2.3 Adjunctions; ⊗\otimes⊗–Hom⁡\HomHom

A universal property relates morphisms of one kind to morphisms of another. When this happens for all objects at once, the two functors involved are adjoint.

Definition 2.8 (Adjunction). Functors F ⁣:C→DF\colon\cat{C}\to\cat{D}F:C→D and G ⁣:D→CG\colon\cat{D}\to\cat{C}G:D→C are adjoint, F⊣GF\dashv GF⊣G, if there are bijections Hom⁡D(F(A),B)≅Hom⁡C(A,G(B)),\begin{aligned} &\Hom_{\cat{D}}(F(A),B)\\ &\quad\cong\Hom_{\cat{C}}(A,G(B)), \end{aligned}​HomD​(F(A),B)≅HomC​(A,G(B)),​ natural in AAA and in BBB. The image of id⁡F(A)\id_{F(A)}idF(A)​ is the unit A→G(F(A))A\to G(F(A))A→G(F(A)); the preimage of id⁡G(B)\id_{G(B)}idG(B)​ is the counit F(G(B))→BF(G(B))\to BF(G(B))→B.

Fix a finite-dimensional space VVV, and write Hom⁡(V,W)\Hom(V,W)Hom(V,W) for the space of linear maps, itself an object of Vectfd\VectfdVectfd​.

Theorem 2.9 (⊗\otimes⊗ and Hom⁡\HomHom are adjoint). The maps Θ ⁣:Hom⁡(U⊗V,W)→Hom⁡(U,Hom⁡(V,W)),\begin{aligned} \Theta\colon&\Hom(U\otimes V,W)\\ &\to\Hom(U,\Hom(V,W)), \end{aligned}Θ:​Hom(U⊗V,W)→Hom(U,Hom(V,W)),​ Θ(f)(u)(v)=f(u⊗v),\Theta(f)(u)(v)=f(u\otimes v),Θ(f)(u)(v)=f(u⊗v), are bijections, natural in UUU and in WWW. So −⊗V⊣Hom⁡(V,−)-\otimes V\dashv\Hom(V,-)−⊗V⊣Hom(V,−) on Vectfd\VectfdVectfd​.

Proof. Θ(f)(u)\Theta(f)(u)Θ(f)(u) is linear in vvv, and Θ(f)\Theta(f)Θ(f) is linear in uuu, because fff is linear and ⊗\otimes⊗ is bilinear. Conversely, for g ⁣:U→Hom⁡(V,W)g\colon U\to\Hom(V,W)g:U→Hom(V,W) the map (u,v)↦g(u)(v)(u,v)\mapsto g(u)(v)(u,v)↦g(u)(v) is bilinear, so by the universal property it is f(u⊗v)f(u\otimes v)f(u⊗v) for exactly one linear fff, and Θ(f)=g\Theta(f)=gΘ(f)=g. Since the u⊗vu\otimes vu⊗v span U⊗VU\otimes VU⊗V, fff is determined by Θ(f)\Theta(f)Θ(f), so Θ\ThetaΘ is a bijection.

Naturality in UUU: for a ⁣:U′→Ua\colon U'\to Ua:U′→U, put f′=f∘(a⊗id⁡V)f'=f\circ(a\otimes\id_V)f′=f∘(a⊗idV​); then Θ(f′)(u′)(v)=f(a(u′)⊗v)=(Θ(f)∘a)(u′)(v).\begin{aligned} \Theta(f')(u')(v)&=f(a(u')\otimes v)\\ &=\bigl(\Theta(f)\circ a\bigr)(u')(v). \end{aligned}Θ(f′)(u′)(v)​=f(a(u′)⊗v)=(Θ(f)∘a)(u′)(v).​ Naturality in WWW: for b ⁣:W→W′b\colon W\to W'b:W→W′, Θ(b∘f)(u)=b∘Θ(f)(u)\Theta(b\circ f)(u)=b\circ\Theta(f)(u)Θ(b∘f)(u)=b∘Θ(f)(u), both sending vvv to b(f(u⊗v))b(f(u\otimes v))b(f(u⊗v)). 

The same pattern in Set\SetSet is currying: a function X×B→CX\times B\to CX×B→C of two variables is a function of the first with values in functions of the second, so −×B⊣(−)B-\times B\dashv(-)^{B}−×B⊣(−)B; for finite sets both sides have ∣C∣∣X∣∣B∣\abs{C}^{\abs{X}\abs{B}}∣C∣∣X∣∣B∣ elements.

The unit and the counit of Theorem 2.9 are maps every physicist has used: the unit U→Hom⁡(V,U⊗V)U\to\Hom(V,U\otimes V)U→Hom(V,U⊗V) and the counit Hom⁡(V,W)⊗V→W\Hom(V,W)\otimes V\to WHom(V,W)⊗V→W, u↦(v↦u⊗v),g⊗v↦g(v).\begin{gathered} u\mapsto(v\mapsto u\otimes v),\\ g\otimes v\mapsto g(v). \end{gathered}u↦(v↦u⊗v),g⊗v↦g(v).​ They satisfy the two triangle identities that every adjunction satisfies (Leinster Lemma 2.2.2): inserting a vector and evaluating gives back what one started with, u⊗v↦(v′↦u⊗v′)⊗v↦u⊗v,g↦(v↦g⊗v)↦(v↦g(v))=g.\begin{gathered} u\otimes v\mapsto(v'\mapsto u\otimes v')\otimes v\mapsto u\otimes v,\\ g\mapsto(v\mapsto g\otimes v)\mapsto(v\mapsto g(v))=g . \end{gathered}u⊗v↦(v′↦u⊗v′)⊗v↦u⊗v,g↦(v↦g⊗v)↦(v↦g(v))=g.​ The counit is evaluation. For W=CW=\CW=C it is the pairing V∗⊗V→CV^{*}\otimes V\to\CV∗⊗V→C, φ⊗v↦φ(v)\varphi\otimes v\mapsto\varphi(v)φ⊗v↦φ(v), which Lecture 4 calls ev⁡V\ev_VevV​ and draws as a cap; there the adjunction becomes the bending of a wire.

2.4 Linear and semisimple categories; Schur

From now on the categories have the structure of Vectfd\VectfdVectfd​ and Rep(G)\Rep(G)Rep(G): morphisms can be added and scaled.

Definition 2.10 (Linear category). A C\CC-linear category has a complex vector space structure on each Hom⁡(X,Y)\Hom(X,Y)Hom(X,Y), with composition bilinear. It is additive if it has a zero object and a biproduct of any two objects.

Vectfd\VectfdVectfd​, Rep(G)\Rep(G)Rep(G) and MatC\catname{Mat}_{\C}MatC​ are C\CC-linear and additive. So is Vectfd n\Vectfd^{\,n}Vectfdn​, whose objects are nnn-tuples (V1,…,Vn)(V_1,\dots,V_n)(V1​,…,Vn​) of finite-dimensional spaces and whose morphisms are nnn-tuples of linear maps, composed and added slot by slot.

Definition 2.11 (Semisimple category). A C\CC-linear additive category with finite-dimensional Hom⁡\HomHom spaces is semisimple, with simple objects X1,…,XnX_1,\dots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​, all nonzero, if

  • (S1)

    Hom⁡(Xi,Xj)=0\Hom(X_i,X_j)=0Hom(Xi​,Xj​)=0 for i≠ji\neq ji=j, and Hom⁡(Xi,Xi)=C id⁡\Hom(X_i,X_i)=\C\,\idHom(Xi​,Xi​)=Cid;

  • (S2)

    every object is isomorphic to a finite direct sum of copies of the XiX_iXi​.

For Rep(G)\Rep(G)Rep(G) with GGG finite, (S1) is Schur’s lemma and (S2) is Maschke’s theorem (Symmetries Thms. 4.12–4.13 and 4.8), and there are as many irreducible representations as conjugacy classes (Symmetries Thm. 5.12). Rep(SU(2))\Rep(\SU(2))Rep(SU(2)), with continuous representations, satisfies both conditions for its irreducible representations, one for each spin j=0,12,1,…j=0,\tfrac12,1,\dotsj=0,21​,1,… (Symmetries Cor. 12.5, Thm. 14.3 and Schur, Thms. 4.12–4.13), but there are infinitely many of them: it is semisimple only in the wider sense that allows an infinite list. We take (S1) and (S2) as the definition, since they are all that is used.

Lemma 2.12 (Multiplicities). If X≅⨁iXi⊕miX\cong\bigoplus_i X_i^{\oplus m_i}X≅⨁i​Xi⊕mi​​, then mi=dim⁡Hom⁡(Xi,X)m_i=\dim\Hom(X_i,X)mi​=dimHom(Xi​,X). So the multiplicities do not depend on the decomposition.

Proof. The Hom⁡\HomHom formula is full faithfulness, followed by Hom⁡(A,B)≅B⊗A∗≅A∗⊗B\Hom(A,B)\cong B\otimes A^{*}\cong A^{*}\otimes BHom(A,B)≅B⊗A∗≅A∗⊗B (Linear Algebra Prop. 12.18). Its dimension is ∑ikimi\sum_i k_im_i∑i​ki​mi​. 

Example 2.14 (Endomorphisms with multiplicity). In Rep(S3)\Rep(S_3)Rep(S3​) let X=1⊕1⊕stdX=\mathbf 1\oplus\mathbf 1\oplus\mathrm{std}X=1⊕1⊕std, with 1\mathbf11 trivial and std\mathrm{std}std the standard representation on the sum-zero plane (Symmetries §4.4). The theorem gives End⁡(X)≅M2(C)×C,\End(X)\cong M_2(\C)\times\C ,End(X)≅M2​(C)×C, of dimension 22+12=52^2+1^2=522+12=5: any linear map between the two trivial summands is an intertwiner, and on std\mathrm{std}std only scalars are. Multiplicity two makes End⁡(X)\End(X)End(X) non-commutative.

The theorem answers the second half of the question: up to equivalence, a semisimple category is a list of vector spaces, one for each simple object, and a morphism is a list of matrices. Only the number of simple objects survives. The abelian group Z3\Z_3Z3​ also has three irreducible representations, the characters k↦ωjkk\mapsto\omega^{jk}k↦ωjk with ω=e2πi/3\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}ω=e2πi/3, so Rep(S3)≃Rep(Z3)≃Vectfd 3\Rep(S_3)\simeq\Rep(\Z_3)\simeq\Vectfd^{\,3}Rep(S3​)≃Rep(Z3​)≃Vectfd3​ as linear categories, although S3S_3S3​ is not abelian and has a two-dimensional irreducible representation. The linear category cannot tell the two groups apart. What can is the tensor product of representations, which Lecture 3 adds to the structure, and the dimensions of the underlying spaces, which an equivalence is free to forget.

Semisimplicity is a hypothesis, and it can fail. Maschke’s theorem needs GGG finite.

Example 2.16 (A Jordan block). Let Z\ZZ act on C2\C^2C2 by ρ(k)=(1k01),\rho(k)=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{pmatrix},ρ(k)=(10​k1​), a representation since ρ(k+l)=ρ(k)ρ(l)\rho(k+l)=\rho(k)\rho(l)ρ(k+l)=ρ(k)ρ(l) (Symmetries Ex. 4.9). The line Ce1\C e_1Ce1​ is invariant. An invariant complement would be a second invariant line, an eigenline of ρ(1)\rho(1)ρ(1); but ρ(1)\rho(1)ρ(1) has the single eigenvalue 111 with the single eigenline Ce1\C e_1Ce1​. So C2\C^2C2 is not a direct sum of two nonzero representations: it is indecomposable. Nor does it satisfy (S1) on its own, since End⁡(C2)\End(\C^2)End(C2) contains ρ(1)−I≠0\rho(1)-I\neq0ρ(1)−I=0 besides the scalars. The fusion categories of Lecture 5 are semisimple by definition, which excludes such examples; every count of simple objects and every multiplicity from there on rests on (S1) and (S2).

Summary

  • Universal properties: terminal objects, products, coproducts and the tensor product are determined up to a unique isomorphism compatible with the universal maps (Lemma 2.2 and Proposition 2.7); in Vect\VectVect the direct sum is a biproduct, and morphisms between biproducts are matrices of morphisms, (2.1).
  • Adjunction: Hom⁡(U⊗V,W)≅Hom⁡(U,Hom⁡(V,W))\Hom(U\otimes V,W)\cong\Hom(U,\Hom(V,W))Hom(U⊗V,W)≅Hom(U,Hom(V,W)) naturally, so −⊗V⊣Hom⁡(V,−)-\otimes V\dashv\Hom(V,-)−⊗V⊣Hom(V,−), with evaluation as the counit (Theorem 2.9).
  • The main result: a semisimple category with simple objects X1,…,XnX_1,\dots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​ is equivalent to Vectfd n\Vectfd^{\,n}Vectfdn​, and dim⁡Hom⁡(X,Y)=∑imi(X)mi(Y)\dim\Hom(X,Y)=\sum_i m_i(X)m_i(Y)dimHom(X,Y)=∑i​mi​(X)mi​(Y) (Theorem 2.13); Rep(S3)\Rep(S_3)Rep(S3​) has three simples, and C3≅1⊕std\C^3\cong\mathbf 1\oplus\mathrm{std}C3≅1⊕std.
  • The guard: a Jordan block in Rep(Z)\Rep(\Z)Rep(Z) is indecomposable but not simple, so semisimplicity can fail (Example 2.16).
